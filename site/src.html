Dutch infrastructure procurement screen

This site reads Dutch infrastructure tenders from 2026 and points out which projects look like a good fit for a capital-projects adviser, and whether it is still early enough for advice to make a difference.

It does not show that an authority will hire advisers, and contract size plays no part in the score. Each project is scored on the four areas of advisory work shown here.

Investment planningbusiness case, feasibility, phasing, programme choices
Procurement & commercialcontract strategy, lotting, risk allocation, market response
Controls & assurancecost, schedule, risk and interfaces in delivery
Asset & lifecyclerenewal, maintenance and life-extension of existing assets
From 456 search results to 18 shortlisted projects. The Method section explains each step.

Three findings

Sheet 01 · findings
FINDING 1 · WHO IS STILL DECIDING

Water boards, grid companies and transport operators are still deciding. Rijkswaterstaat has already decided.

Rijkswaterstaat still has open questions, but they don't show up here. Since July 2025 it hires project-control and contract-management advice from a fixed pool of firms (the SROK PPB 2 framework), so that work goes straight to the pool and never appears as a public tender.

FINDING 2 · CHANGE IN DELIVERY

Renovating bridges, locks and barriers while they stay in use leads to repeated contract changes

Some notices give the reason for the change. Pipe brackets on the Oosterscheldekering had to be replaced after a TNO study. The Krammer locks needed extra work for radio-coverage problems, possibly caused by a nearby wind farm. Structures at Terbregseplein needed repairs that fell outside the A16 contract.

Managing these changes, by checking whether they are justified, keeping cost and schedule under control and handling claims, is a core part of project-controls advice.

FINDING 3 · GRID CONSTRAINTS

Grid companies are struggling to buy enough construction work

The notices do show pressure on the companies building the grid. TenneT raised one contractor framework from €800m to €1.4bn, increased a second, and made its cable and line contractors re-apply under new schemes. Stedin launched an eight-year programme for high-voltage routes, valued at €385m. In this data, the grid problem looks like a buying problem: securing enough contractors, on the right contracts, with the right risk split.

The shortlist

18 of 154 projects · evidence ≥ 2.5

How the ranking works

Every project in the sample gets a priority score. The score asks two things: how well does the problem in the notice match the work a capital-projects adviser does, and is it still early enough for advice to make a difference? Projects that fit well and are still being decided rank highest. The 18 highest form the shortlist.

Contract size is not used. Full method ↓

Fit
Each project is scored 1–5 on investment planning, procurement & commercial, controls & assurance, and asset & lifecycle. The four are averaged into a fit score out of 100.
Timing
Early projects keep their full score. Projects in tender lose 20%; awarded or running ones lose 30%. There is less left to influence.
Evidence
Thin notices lose up to 30%. Projects below 2.5 out of 5 on evidence are excluded.
Priority
The score after both adjustments. No project scores 5 on all four areas, so around 50 is the top of the range.
Stability
The ranking was re-run with seven different weightings. The badge shows how many times the project stayed in the top 18. 7/7 means all seven.

For none of the 18 projects was an adviser found already hired. Rijkswaterstaat projects get advice through its SROK PPB 2 framework instead. Values come from the official notice where the authority stated one.

    Method & validation

    Sheet 05 · how the screen was built
    reproducible · 11 Python scripts

    How this was made

    I built this study with an AI agent (Claude, by Anthropic). The agent collected the notices, wrote the Python pipeline and proposed the scoring method. I set the question and scope, reviewed the agent's scoring decisions, and checked the findings against the data and the official notices, correcting claims that did not hold. All code and data are in the repository, so every step can be traced.

    Where the data comes from

    Every Dutch public body has to announce larger purchases on TenderNed, the government's tender website. This study uses notices published there between 1 January and 14 October 2026. The European database (TED) could not be reached from the analysis environment, but TenderNed republishes Dutch European notices, so they are covered: 154 of the 183 procedures carry a European reference number.

    Notices were found by searching category by category: energy, transport, water, renewal and advisory work. From there, the sample was narrowed in five steps:

    • 456 search results. Some notices came up in more than one search.
    • 411 unique notices after removing the repeats.
    • 287 procedures. One purchase can produce several notices over its life (announcement, award, later changes), so notices were grouped by the purchase they belong to.
    • 183 in scope. 104 procedures were dropped because they were not infrastructure: schools, hospitals, offices, housing, ICT advice and similar. Each decision is logged.
    • 154 projects. Some procedures are pieces of the same problem, such as five separate contracts on the Oosterscheldekering. The agent grouped these into 13 clusters, which I checked; the other 141 projects are single procedures.

    The notice descriptions were translated and summarised in English by a language model during collection. Names, dates and codes were copied exactly and checked against the official documents.

    How projects are scored

    Fit. Each project is scored 1 to 5 on the four areas. Scoring happens in two passes. First, automatic rules look for signs in the notice text. A market consultation raises investment planning, a competitive dialogue raises procurement, work on a bridge that stays open raises controls. Sentences that rule something out ("project management is not included") are removed first, so they don't count. Then the agent read every record and adjusted 187 scores where the rules missed something, each with a one-line reason. 74.5% of scores were left unchanged. Most changes raised a score, because rules only catch what a notice says literally. Both the automatic and the reviewed score are kept, and both appear in each project's dossier.

    Investment planning

    Scores high when the scope, phasing or business case is still open, or a later phase is not yet committed.

    Procurement & commercial

    Scores high for market consultations, competitive dialogue, build teams, lotting choices, frameworks and re-tenders.

    Controls & assurance

    Scores high for work in assets that stay in use, many interfaces, fixed deadlines and multi-contract programmes.

    Asset & lifecycle

    Scores high for renewal, life-extension and maintenance of critical existing assets.

    The four scores are averaged into a fit score from 0 to 100.

    Timing. The earlier a project is, the more room advice has to shape it. Projects still before the tender keep their full score. A cancelled tender that will be re-run loses 10%, a project in tender 20%, and a project already awarded or under way 30%.

    Evidence. Some notices say very little. A project's evidence score (1 to 5) reflects how much the notice describes, how precisely it is located and how many details it gives. Thin evidence lowers the score by up to 30%. Projects scoring below 2.5 are left off the shortlist.

    Priority. Fit × timing × evidence. The adjustments multiply, so a high score in one place cannot make up for weak evidence in another. Because no project scores 5 on all four areas, around 50 is the top of the range. Contract size is not used: only 4 of 183 notices state a value, and a small early question can matter more than a large contract that is already decided.

    How solid is the ranking

    Giving each area the same weight is a choice. To test it, the ranking was re-run under seven settings: equal weights, each area given extra weight in turn, fit only, and timing counting extra. Eight projects stay in the top 18 under all seven. The settings that move the list most are asset emphasis and fit only, because they favour large renovations already under way over early strategy questions. The Explorer lets you try these yourself.

    How it was checked

    The 18 shortlisted notices, plus 8 picked at random, were compared field by field with the official TenderNed documents: title, authority, notice type, category code, location and date. Every field that could be compared matched. Location could be compared for 14 of the 26; for the others, the notice data or the official document names no region. Dates sometimes differ by a day or two, which is the gap between when a notice is sent and when TenderNed publishes it. One detail differed in substance: Stedin's notice lists three lots where earlier summaries said two. The official document is used.

    Limits

    • A sample, not the whole market. Searches returned the newest notices first, so May to October is better covered than January to April.
    • The need for advice is inferred. The score says the problem fits, not that the authority will hire advisers. It is confirmed only where a notice buys advice directly.
    • Descriptions are AI summaries. Codes and names were checked against the originals; the summaries themselves were not.
    • Scoring was not independently double-checked. The review was done once, by the agent with my checks; no independent second reviewer was used.
    • Groupings are a judgement call. Different clusters would shift some ranks.
    • No site locations and no grid data. No notice gives a site address, and the grid operators' capacity map could not be retrieved.
    • Delivery risk and grid dependence were assessed from the notices but kept out of the score. Delivery risk is not shown on this site because most notices do not describe it; the data is in the repository.
    • Private and semi-public owners (Schiphol, grid and heat companies) publish only what procurement law requires.
